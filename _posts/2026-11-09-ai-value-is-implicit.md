The majority of companies around the world are asking themselves how they can use AI to go faster, build better products or improve yields. Some of those companies mean LLMs while others mean traditional ML. What all of them believe they'll get is "value". They see their peers claiming things are faster or better with AI and believe they have to do the same. They start spending millions in inference costs, tokens flying everywhere. No one has an AI budget - yet. The bill lands, finance asks what they got for that money and leadership looks around, licks their finger and raises it to the wind. No one is measuring the value from AI, the bill is the only proof.

I started in the computer vision ML space just as the transformer architecture that powers LLMs [was being designed](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762). Fast forward to today and we have LLMs with [trillions of parameters](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Pro) while the original model behind ChatGPT was based on GPT-3's [175 Billion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165). Since then it seems like every industry has been scrambling to integrate "AI" (read LLMs) into their business: chatbots for customer service, AI-generated documentation, corporate knowledge bases, generated code, branding and marketing content, "smart" suggestions, crude but quick classifiers, the list goes on. We're generating tokens like they're an infinite resource and paying through the nose to make sure we're getting the "best" arrangement of those tokens.

## What Is Value?

When we talk about value here, we're really talking about business value and that falls into a few categories: creating something new, optimizing something that already exists, reducing headcount, and doing more with the same headcount. These are all the things we've been promised AI will do for us. If you read [OpenAI's principles](https://openai.com/index/our-principles/), they all sound quite noble, forward-thinking and - depending on how much you believe - almost utopian. Machines do the work, humans prosper. We aren't there yet, we haven't reached AGI and I believe most companies aren't accurately measuring the value (or lack thereof) AI is delivering today. Every AI output carries some level of human tax: reviewing, fixing, or maintaining. The promise is that AI accelerates, but it just shifts where the time goes.

## The Reckoning

I think we have seen the first (but not the last) of the AI reckoning. Shareholders of major corporations reacted to earnings last month [eroding $2T in market value](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/2-trillion-wiped-why-u-160355598.html) within just a couple days. As the article states "The question is no longer whether AI is real; it is whether the math works." Shareholders aren't seeing the return on investment that all the AI spend is supposedly bringing or going to bring, and they're tired of waiting for it to pay off. This is what we're seeing right now in the public market. What I'm worried about is what's going to happen in private markets: investors are soon going to stop footing the bill for this unmonitored free rein token spend, unless companies can prove the value they're getting for these tokens.

## An Example of Value

We recently had a task at work to refactor an eventing system based on a really well considered, documented and defined taxonomy. The ask from our data team was to migrate all existing events to the new system. 3 years ago, we'd have split this up amongst owning teams and come back with an estimate of when we could have it done, based on all the other priorities in flight. I took the code and the taxonomy and gave an agent the task to do it. It fanned out, and within a couple hours and $282 of token spend, it was all converted and ready for production. One not experienced in software development may balk at this price tag, so let's consider it more carefully.

According to Canada's Job Bank, the [median hourly wage for a developer is $48.08](https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/marketreport/wages-occupation/22548/ca). For simplicity, let's call it $48. We estimated this to take 1 developer somewhere around 12 full time hours. $48 * 12 = $576. So it costs a little more than double for a developer to do the task by hand. I still had to prompt, review output and test so let's call it 1 developer hour worked - that's 11 developer hours saved. 11 * $48 = $528. If we then subtract the $282 in token costs we can say we saved (or created value of) $246. If you consider that I was able to complete four other work items that day instead of just the refactor, it's clear to see how AI created value in this scenario.

Before we sign off on the final number, we need to weigh a few additional facts. First, our math suggests there was only one hour of my time, when in reality someone else had to review the work too. Second, $48/hour is a national median, not the market rate for a staff developer in Toronto. Third, by unblocking the event taxonomy, our system now has clean data we can use to evaluate new business opportunities — another value that would never have appeared in an estimate. The fourth, and more important, factor is that this is a rare type of developer task that can easily be estimated: fixed scope, clear definition of done, extraordinarily clear requirements. The four other items I cleared that day were the exact opposite, ambiguous work that developers have struggled [for decades to estimate](https://opencommons.org/CHAOS_Report_on_IT_Project_Outcomes) accurately. It's not that the $246 figure is wrong: it's the only number we can measure for that day. The rest of the value is implicit.

## The Other Side of the Ledger

Now consider another scenario. A customer is on your page with a problem that needs a real human. Instead they get a chatbot. If your company was smart about it, then it's a minor annoyance that the bot can't handle the request and it escalates to a human. In the worst-case scenario, there isn't a clear way to get a human, the chatbot spins them in loops and the customer ends up dissatisfied and a few days later churns. The chatbot is meant to take pressure off support teams and help customers, but is anyone measuring the longer customer journey? In this scenario you've paid to build and run a bot that has cost you a customer. Which spreadsheet does that lost value live on?

Writing code with AI can be much the same as our poor lobotomized chatbot, confidently bad code making its way into production systems. Code that maybe solves a customer problem but is a ticking time bomb because an agent chose to use an integer as a primary key field on a table that holds every database transaction the system ever produced ([or maybe that was a human choice](https://docs.djangoproject.com/en/6.1/ref/models/fields/#django.db.models.AutoField)). While technically correct and functionally correct, the issue comes when no one made the decision. The developer looked at the code (we hope) and said, yeah that's alright. Years later this issue comes to a head and it's not the model that's going to wake up at 3am when the database is screeching to a halt and fix it for us. It's not the maintainer of the package that's going to fix it either; it's the developer.

## Honestly

This was never meant to answer the question, only to make you think about the cost and value of AI. In this post I focused exclusively on the token bill. I didn't mention the societal human costs we are just beginning to see take shape like: [lower cognitive function in AI users](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/), [unmeasured environmental costs of compute](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-25-107172.pdf), or the probable economic downturn when [NVIDIA's shell game falls apart.](https://theconversation.com/whos-paying-whom-the-circular-money-machine-behind-the-ai-boom-or-bubble-293938)

When we have new ways to create value, we probably need new ways to measure value. At the very least we need to refresh our existing methods of measuring to accommodate the new norm. AI systems and infrastructure are growing rapidly, and I don't expect us to keep pace. But if we're going to understand how these tools impact our businesses, it has to be more than vibes, implicit belief, and unhinged LinkedIn posts about how much of your codebase is now written by bots. If we can't measure it, be honest about it: the value is implicit.
